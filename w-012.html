The AI Creative Lab #03: Your AI is guessing what your customers want

Let me guess how you're using AI for ads. Why AI-written hooks sound like everyone else's, the 4 kinds of real data to give your agent first, and 4 prompts that turn that data into briefs.

Let me guess how you're using AI for ads

You open Claude or ChatGPT and type something like: "Write 10 hooks for a Facebook ad for our product. Target audience: busy professionals."

10 hooks come back in seconds. They're clean and confident, and they could run for any brand in your category. "Tired of wasting time on…?" "What if you could finally…?" "Here's the secret nobody tells you…"

You pick the 3 that sound least generic, tweak a few words and send them to the editor. They go live. Hook rate is flat. CPA climbs. The team decides AI "isn't there yet" for ads, and goes back to writing by hand.

Sound familiar?

I made the same mistake when we first brought AI into our copywriting. Product name in, generic hooks out.

My team has shipped 14,513 ad creatives across 5 performance verticals. With AI in the analysis, the scripts and the video, we now ship 200+ a day, and profit on our performance campaigns is up 30%. The models we use are the same ones you have. What changed is everything we give them before they write a word.

What I learnt

An AI model writes ads from what it has. Given only a product name and an audience label, it writes from training data, which means the average of every ad it has seen. The average ad doesn't stop anyone.

It has never seen your account's numbers, your winning scripts or the exact words your customers use. When it doesn't have them, it guesses, and the guesses sound like everyone else.

So the useful question before any prompt is: what real data does the model have right now?

Give your agent 4 kinds of real data

  1. Your performance data. Yesterday's ad-level export, or a live connection to your ad account if your setup has one. This is how the model knows which ads are working.
  2. Your winners, broken down. Your best scripts split into the same 6 parts every time (claim, proof, bonding, setup, objection, story). Issue 01 has the prompt.
  3. Your competitors' live ads. The Meta Ad Library is public and free. Ads that have run for months are usually paying for themselves.
  4. Your customers' own words. Reviews, ad comments, support tickets, sales-call notes and Reddit threads in your category. The phrases people use when they complain are your best hooks.

How to put it in place: 4 steps

1. Find the angles in your customers' words

Paste 50 to 100 reviews, comments or Reddit posts into Claude:

These are real comments and reviews from people in our market.

1. Group them by the problem they describe. Name each group
   in the customer's words, not ours.
2. For each group, quote the 3 most vivid lines word for word.
3. Note the objections and doubts people raise before buying.
4. Rank the groups by how often they appear.

Don't invent quotes. Only use lines from the text below.

[paste comments here]

2. Map what competitors are already running

Search your category in the Meta Ad Library, note the ads that have been live the longest, and paste their copy or transcripts in:

These are competitor ads that have been running for a long time,
with how long each has been live.

For each ad: hook (verbatim), angle, offer, proof used, CTA, format.
Then tell me:
- which angles 3 or more competitors share
- which customer problems from my list above none of them address
- which hooks feel overused in this category

The gap between what customers say and what competitors run is where your next angle comes from.

3. Write the brief before the ad

Using the customer groups, the competitor map and my winning-script
breakdown above, write 3 creative briefs.

Each brief: angle, customer quote it's built on, hook direction,
proof to use, objection to answer, format, and the winner it borrows
structure from.

Only use claims and numbers that appear in the data above.

A brief built this way can be checked. Every line points back to a customer quote, a competitor gap or a proven structure.

4. Draft, then make it human

Ask for scripts from one brief at a time, with 3 hook variants each. Then a person edits: swap in the exact customer phrases, cut anything a real person wouldn't say on camera, and check every claim. In health, finance and insurance, a compliance reviewer reads every line before it ships.

4 mistakes I'd avoid

  • Prompting with a product name and an audience label. That's the guessing setup, every time.
  • Letting the model make up numbers or quotes. Tell it to use only what's in the data, and check that it did.
  • Shipping 50 thin variants. 5 variants from one strong brief teach you more than 50 from a weak one.
  • Skipping the human edit. The model gets you to a solid draft. A person who knows the customer gets it ready to run.
