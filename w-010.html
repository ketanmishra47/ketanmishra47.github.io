The AI Creative Lab #01: You can't scale a winning ad you can't explain

What 14,513 ad creatives taught me about breaking down winners: the mistake that cost us most, the 6-part fix, and the Claude prompt I'd start with.

Length849 words · 4 min read

The AI Creative Lab · Issue 01

Subject: You can't scale a winning ad you can't explain
Preview: What 14,513 creatives taught me about breaking down winners, and the prompt I'd start with

I've written and run ads for 8 years. The last 3 have been in performance marketing, in some of the hardest categories to advertise: personal loans, debt relief, Medicare, ACA and auto insurance.

In that time my team has shipped 14,513 ad creatives. We now ship 200+ a day, with AI in the analysis, the scripts and the video. Profit on our performance campaigns is up 30%.

Most of what made that work, I learnt by getting it wrong first. The AI Creative Lab is where I write those lessons up: what I tried, what broke, and the better way, with a prompt or workflow you can use the same week.

Issue 01 starts with the mistake that cost us the most.

What I got wrong

When an ad won, I studied it, wrote down why I thought it worked and briefed the next variants from my notes. Every strategist on my team did the same.

Then we compared notes. 2 strategists looking at the same winning script described it differently. One credited the hook. The other credited the proof in the middle. Each used their own words for the same patterns.

The details suffered too. Someone would miss what was doing the work, and the next variant rebuilt the wrong part of the ad. We were briefing at volume from notes that didn't agree with each other.

What I learnt

Everyone was describing ads in their own words, so none of the notes could be compared, ranked or reused.

Before a team can copy what works, everyone has to take a winning ad apart the same way. That means agreeing on the format first.

The better way: one breakdown, every time

We agreed on 6 structural elements, and every winning script now gets split into them:

  1. Pure claim. What the ad says the product does.
  2. Pure proof. Why the viewer should believe it.
  3. Bonding. The lines that make the speaker feel like one of the viewer's own.
  4. Setup for payoff. Something planted early that pays off later.
  5. Objection handling. The doubt the ad answers before the viewer says it.
  6. Storytelling. The before and after that holds the body together.

Each script also gets tagged with its hook type, the psychological trigger, the speaker archetype and a compliance flag on any risky line. In health, lending and insurance, that last tag protects the account.

Then hand the breakdown to AI

Once the format was fixed, it could be automated. We built an agentic workflow: Claude reads the scripts, Gemini reads the video frames, and both work from one system-instructions file that defines the 6 elements and the tags.

For every batch of winners it returns:

  • a table per file: niche, hook type, archetype, distinctive element
  • every hook in the batch, ranked by how often it appears, with a template line
  • the shared structure with timings, and compliance flags line by line
  • a one-page blueprint, and replica scripts written against it

2 strategists running the pipeline on the same batch now get the same answer. For the first time, our library of winners was something we could sort, rank and brief from.

Try it this week

You can start without building a pipeline. Paste 3 to 5 of your winning scripts into Claude with this prompt:

You are analysing winning direct-response video ads.
For each script below, return a table with these columns:
File | Hook (first line, verbatim) | Hook type | Psychological trigger | Speaker archetype

Then split each script into these 6 elements and quote the lines for each:
1. Pure claim  2. Pure proof  3. Bonding  4. Setup for payoff
5. Objection handling  6. Storytelling

Flag any line that makes a health, financial or legal claim a compliance
reviewer would question, and say why.

Finish with:
- the hook types ranked by how often they appear
- one template line per hook type, with the product details removed
- the structure the winners share, in order, with rough timings

Use the same column names and element names for every script.

[paste scripts here]

4 rules that keep the output useful:

  • Winners only. The pattern you want is the one that already made money.
  • Paste scripts word for word. A paraphrase loses the exact wording that made the hook land.
  • Never change the column names. The value comes from every script being described in the same words.
  • A person reads every compliance flag. The model gives you a first pass. Someone who knows the vertical's rules signs off.
