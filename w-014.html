How to write regulated ads people stop for, without naming their problem

Meta won't let your ad name the viewer's health or money problem, and since 2025 it won't let you target them by age or ZIP either. 4 ways to write hooks that make the right person stop anyway.

Length952 words · 4 min read

Let me guess how your last regulated ad went

You're writing for a debt relief offer. The hook writes itself: "Drowning in credit card debt?"

Meta rejects it.

So you play it safe: "Take control of your financial future today." It gets approved. It also gets a hook rate so low the ad never leaves testing, because nobody scrolling past it recognises themselves in it.

The first version was too direct to run. The second was too vague to work. Most ads in health, lending and insurance die somewhere between those 2 drafts.

Sound familiar?

My team has shipped 14,513 ad creatives across 5 of the strictest categories on Meta: personal loans, debt relief, Medicare, ACA and auto insurance. We now ship 200+ a day in them.

The rejected hook and the bland rewrite are the 2 drafts I see most from writers new to these verticals, and I wrote plenty of both before I learnt where the line sits. This is how to write between them.

What the rules say

2 rules shape every ad in these categories.

1. You can't tell the viewer what's wrong with them

Meta's personal attributes policy bars ads that assert or imply a viewer's personal attributes. Physical and mental health and vulnerable financial status are both on the list.

Meta's own examples make the line clear. "Do you have diabetes?" is not allowed. "New diabetes treatment available" is. "Are you bankrupt? Check out our services." is not allowed.

The word "you" is fine. What breaks the rule is a line that tells the viewer they have the condition or the money problem.

2. You can't pick the audience the way you used to

Since January 2025, US ads for credit, insurance, bank accounts and other financial services have to run under Meta's Financial Products and Services special ad category. That category takes away age, gender and ZIP-code targeting and lookalike audiences.

Put the 2 rules together and the job changes. The copy can't name the viewer's problem, and the targeting can't find the viewer for you. The creative has to do both: find the right person and make them recognise themselves, without saying who they are.

What I learnt: let them recognise themselves

A viewer stops when they see their own situation. They don't need to be told they're in it. 4 ways to write that:

1. Let the speaker tell their own story

An ad can't say the viewer has $14,000 on 3 credit cards. A creator in a UGC ad can say what happened to them: "I had 3 cards maxed out and I was only paying the interest." The viewer who's in the same spot recognises it in a second. The line describes the speaker, so nobody is being told what's wrong with them.

Keep it honest. If the story is a dramatisation, the creator isn't a real customer, and the claims still need to match what the product does.

2. Lead with the news

Meta's own allowed example is a news line: "New diabetes treatment available." The same shape works across these verticals. A new plan year, a new program, a rate change, a deadline. News gives people a reason to stop that has nothing to do with being singled out.

3. Use formats that carry the doubt for the viewer

Skeptic tests and creator confessions let a person on screen voice the doubt the viewer has. "I didn't believe this was real, so I checked" pulls in the person who's been burned before, and it never mentions their history.

4. Let the details do the targeting

With age and ZIP targeting gone, specificity is how the right people self-select. A hook about juggling 3 card due dates finds a different viewer from a hook about a first car loan. Write the moment so precisely that the right person feels it's about them, and everyone else scrolls on.

Every line in the last column still needs the offer to back it up. A better hook doesn't change what the product can promise.

How to put it in place

  1. Write the rejected hook first, on purpose. It tells you exactly what the viewer is feeling. Then rewrite it as a story, a piece of news or a format that carries the feeling without naming the person.
  2. Check every hook against one question. Does this line tell the viewer something about their health or money? If yes, rewrite it.
  3. Put the targeting in the creative. Brief 4 hooks for 4 specific moments, and let the results show which audience each one finds.
  4. Keep a person on compliance. AI can flag risky lines in seconds. Someone who knows the vertical still reads every flagged line, and every claim, before anything ships.

The ads that work in these categories are specific enough that the right person stops, and careful enough that nobody is told who they are.
